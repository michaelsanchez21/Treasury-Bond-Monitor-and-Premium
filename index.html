Duration and the 10-year term premium since 1990

Modified duration says how much a 10-year Treasury’s price moves for a one-point change in its yield. The term premium is the part of that yield that pays investors for locking money up for ten years rather than rolling short bills. Duration is computed on this page from the daily 10-year yield.

Method and sources

Duration. The 10-year yield is treated as the yield on a 10-year Treasury trading at par with semiannual coupons. Its modified duration is D = [1 − (1 + y/2)−20] / y, with y the yield as a decimal. At a 4% yield that gives about 8.2 years. A 10-year zero-coupon bond, the instrument the term premium is defined on, would have a longer duration of 10 / (1 + y/2).

Term premium. The Kim and Wright (2005) three-factor arbitrage-free model, published daily by the Federal Reserve Board. It is a model estimate, not a traded price. The New York Fed’s ACM model gives a different level but similar moves in this episode.

Price effect of a term-premium change. First-order approximation: ΔP/P ≈ −D × Δ(term premium). It isolates the part of the price change caused by the term premium, holding expected short rates fixed.

Window changes. Each end of a window uses the last available observation on or before that date, so weekends and holidays are handled the same way for every series. Dollar changes are log changes in percent.

Baseline. Using Friday-to-Friday changes over the three years before the window starts, the page estimates the usual link between the dollar and the term premium: their correlation and the slope from regressing the weekly dollar change on the weekly term-premium change. The slope times the window’s term-premium change is the dollar move the baseline would have predicted. The surprise is the actual move minus that prediction, divided by the baseline’s residual standard deviation scaled to the window’s length.

Limits. Two series moving together over one week does not establish cause. The tariff announcements moved growth expectations, Fed expectations and risk appetite at the same time, and the dollar responds to all of them.

Data. THREEFYTP10, DGS10 and DTWEXBGS from FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. The model paper: Kim and Wright (2005). Data downloaded from FRED on November 10, 2026.
